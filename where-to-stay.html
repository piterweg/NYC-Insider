Where to stay

Where to stay in New York

The right base is not the best hotel in the city — it is the one that puts your evenings within a short ride of your bed. Here are eleven areas compared honestly, what the tax really adds, and how to book without overpaying.

11 areas comparedPrices before taxSubway-first adviceChecked November 2026
How to choose

Choose the line before the hotel

Start with the two or three places you will actually spend your evenings, then find a station on a line that reaches them. In New York the address matters less than the journey from it.

A first trip usually wants Midtown for convenience, or the Upper West Side for calm and park mornings. A second trip can trade a little travel time for better food, bigger rooms and lower rates in Brooklyn, Queens or Jersey City. The rule that saves trips

Pick a hotel within a five-minute walk of a station on a line you will use twice a day. A plain building in the right place beats a beautiful one further out.

☂

Noise is the variable nobody checks

Midtown, SoHo and Williamsburg are lively at night. If you sleep lightly, ask for a room away from the street and read reviews that mention noise.

What the tax adds

New York adds roughly 14.75% hotel tax plus $3.50 per night on top of the quoted rate, so a $400 room is closer to $462 before you tip a porter. Ask for the all-in total — room, tax, occupancy fee and any facility fee — before comparing two hotels.

The same room, three prices

The hotel's own site, a booking site and a package can all quote different totals for the same night. Compare the final number, including tax and fees.

Booking

How to book a New York hotel

The rate is only half the decision. These are the things that change what you actually pay and how well you sleep.

Direct or agent

Book direct when the rate matches

Hotel websites usually match the large booking sites and are easier to change if a flight moves. Compare on an agent site, then check the same dates on the hotel's own site and take the better cancellation terms.

Cancellation

Read the cut-off date

Flexible rates cost a little more and are worth it in New York, where plans change. Note the exact date and hour you can cancel by — some are 72 hours, some three days before arrival.

Room size

Expect a small room

New York hotel rooms are small, and “queen” describes the bed rather than the space. Check the floor plan and whether the window opens.

Fees

Watch for facility fees

Some hotels add a nightly resort, destination or facility fee for the gym, Wi-Fi or a bar credit. It is not covered by the tax line, so ask what is compulsory before you book.

Location

Location beats facilities

A rooftop pool matters less than the walk to the subway. Check the nearest station and which lines serve it, then count the stops to the places you will visit.

Questions

Where to stay — common questions

Which area is best for a first visit?

Midtown, unless noise and crowds bother you. It is the only area where the Empire State Building, Times Square, Bryant Park, Grand Central and the theatres are all within a walk. The Upper West Side is the calmer alternative, with the park and the American Museum of Natural History on the doorstep.

Is Brooklyn a good base for a first trip?

It can be. Downtown Brooklyn is around ten minutes from Lower Manhattan on several lines, and Williamsburg has the best food and nightlife of any outer-borough base. You trade some convenience for space and value — see the Brooklyn guide.

Is Jersey City cheaper, and what is the catch?

Usually cheaper, with newer hotels and a fast PATH ride to the World Trade Center or 33rd Street. The catch is that PATH stops overnight, so a late evening means a taxi.

How much is hotel tax, and what else is added?

Roughly 14.75% hotel tax plus $3.50 per night, on top of the quoted rate, and some hotels add a compulsory facility fee as well. See the budget guide for the rest of the picture.

Should I tip hotel staff?

Yes, modestly. Around $2–5 per bag for the porter and $3–5 per night for housekeeping is the local norm. Doormen and concierges are tipped for a specific service, not routinely.
